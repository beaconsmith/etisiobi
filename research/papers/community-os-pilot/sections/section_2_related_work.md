# Section 2: Related Work — DSR in the Informal Sector

The development of the Oroma Coordination OS sits at the intersection of Design Science Research (DSR) and ICT for Development (ICT4D). This section situates the work within the DSR paradigm and identifies the gap in current community governance literature.

## 2.1 Foundations of Design Science Research
We adopt the DSR guidelines established by Hevner et al. (2004), which prioritize the creation of "innovative, purposeful artifacts" that solve relevant business (or in our case, institutional) problems. Following Gregor and Hevner (2013), this artifact is classified as a **Level 2 Contribution (Exaptation)**: applying known design principles (blockchain, state machines, hybrid databases) to a new problem domain (informal community governance).

## 2.2 Digital Transformation Beyond the State
Mainstream e-government research (Heeks 2017, Misuraca 2021) primarily focuses on the digitalization of formal state bureaucracies. While successful in high-resource environments, these models often fail when applied to the Global South because they ignore the "Informal Gap"—the reality that significant governance functions occur outside of formal state structures (Muwafu 2024). Oroma addresses this by treating the community institution itself, rather than the state, as the primary design client.

## 2.3 Coordination Theory in Informal Institutions
Existing coordination tools (e.g., Slack, Trello, DAOs) assume either a corporate hierarchy or a fully decentralized digital-native population. However, community institutions in Southeast Nigeria operate in a "hybrid" space: they are deeply hierarchical and rule-bound yet lack formal digital infrastructure. Literature on polycentric governance (Ostrom 1990) suggests that successful coordination in these settings requires "institutional legibility"—the ability of the institution to represent its rules and state in a format that is verifiable by its members.

## 2.4 The Research Gap: Artifact-First Trust
While previous research has explored the social capital of town unions (Nwosu 2009, Chuku 2015), there is a lack of design science literature focusing on the **technical stabilization** of these institutions. Most interventions are social or policy-based; Oroma represents a shift toward an "Artifact-First" approach, where the digital tool itself acts as the anchor for institutional memory and trust.
