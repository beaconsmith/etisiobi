# Section 1: Introduction — From Recall to Receipts

Trust is the currency of community governance. In Southeast Nigeria, town unions and village assemblies function effectively because members trust the shared norms and social recall of the community. However, as these institutions scale and engage with more complex development projects, the limitations of "social recall" become apparent. Conflicting memories of meeting minutes, opaque treasury state transitions, and "zombie proposals" create a "Trust Deficit" that often leads to institutional stagnation or leadership crisis.

This paper proposes the **Artifact-First Trust** model: a shift from relying on what people *remember* to what a digital system *records*. We argue that the creation of verifiable digital artifacts (receipts, signatures, hashing) is the most effective way to stabilize trust in informal community governance.

## 1.1 The Problem: The Recall Deficit
Informal institutions are vulnerable to "informational drift." Without a verifiable record, the powerful can re-interpret past decisions to suit current interests. This "Recall Deficit" is not a failure of character, but a failure of infrastructure. To achieve high-integrity governance, communities need a digital foundation that makes decisions "artifact-based" rather than "memory-based."

## 1.2 Objective
The objective of this research is to define the principles of Artifact-First Trust and demonstrate how they can be applied to community governance. We ask: *Can digital artifacts act as a surrogate for formal state oversight in informal settings?*

## 1.3 Contribution
This paper contributes to the digital governance literature by:
1. Synthesizing records management (ISO 15489) and procedural justice (Tyler 2006) for the informal sector.
2. Proposing four "Artifact-First" design principles (Section 3).
3. Demonstrating how "Receipts over Recall" can provide a non-repudiable foundation for community trust.

By prioritizing the artifact, we provide a path for informal institutions to build the "institutional memory" necessary for long-term development success.
