# Section 3: Theoretical Framework

To understand how digital records facilitate trust in community governance, we propose a framework that shifts the locus of institutional legitimacy from **personal recall** to **verifiable artifacts**. This framework is built on three pillars: Procedural Justice, Records as Social Artifacts, and the reduction of Information Asymmetry.

## 3.1 Procedural Justice and the "Evidence of Fairness"
We adopt Tom Tyler's (2006) theory of **Procedural Justice**, which posits that the primary driver of institutional trust is the perceived fairness of the decision-making process, rather than the outcomes themselves. In community governance, this perception is highly vulnerable to "recall bias" and accusations of favoritism. We argue that **artifacts**—non-repudiable, time-stamped digital receipts of votes, contributions, and decisions—serve as the "evidence of fairness" that enables procedural justice to scale beyond face-to-face interactions.

## 3.2 Records as Social Artifacts
We draw on Eghweree (2021) to conceptualize records not as passive repositories of data, but as **active social artifacts** that mediate the relationship between citizens and local governance structures. In the context of Nigerian local administration, Eghweree argues that the failure of trust often stems from the "unverifiability" of administrative actions. By adopting an **Artifact-First** approach (drawing on standards like ISO 15489 and SCITT), we treat every governance event as a discrete, verifiable object that exists independently of the actor's testimony, thereby bridging the "trust gap" in informal associations.

## 3.3 Mitigating Information Asymmetry
Community governance often suffers from extreme **Information Asymmetry** (Akerlof 1970) between the executive committee (the agents) and the general membership (the principals). Without verifiable records, the membership must rely on the "good faith" of the leadership—a fragile basis for trust in high-stakes communal projects (e.g., land disputes or isusu management). The transition to an artifact-first system transforms the "Black Box" of communal decision-making into a transparent ledger, reducing the agency costs of community participation.

## 3.4 From Recall-Based to Artifact-Based Trust
Finally, we theorize the shift from **Recall-Based Trust** (typical of oral-tradition institutions) to **Artifact-Based Trust**. While recall-based trust is highly resilient in small, homogeneous groups, it fails to scale as communities become more geographically dispersed and socio-economically diverse. Artifact-based trust, powered by digital-science demonstrations of verifiability, provides a new substrate for community legitimacy that can withstand the pressures of modern, digitally-mediated collective action.
