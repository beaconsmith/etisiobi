# Section 4: Empirical Evidence — The Case of Southeast Nigeria

This section provides empirical grounding for the paper's framework, drawing on secondary data from academic studies and governance reports in Southeast Nigeria. We examine both the immense governance capacity of town unions and the critical failure modes that necessitate digital stabilization.

## 4.1 Governance Capacity: The "Principal Agent" of Development
Studies consistently identify town unions as the primary engines of rural community development in Igboland (SFJESGS 2021). 
- **Infrastructure Provision:** Town unions have historically built over 70% of the rural infrastructure in certain communities, including schools, health centers, and feeder roads (Nwosu 2009).
- **Dispute Resolution:** The *Nzuko-Arochukwu* case study (Okafor 2020) demonstrates how community institutions resolve land and family disputes more effectively and at a lower cost than formal state courts.
- **Collective Action:** ROSCAs and community saving schemes operate at significant scale, providing financial liquidity in environments with low formal banking penetration (AESS 2023).

## 4.2 Accountability Failures: The "Recall Trap"
Despite their successes, town unions are prone to administrative instability due to their reliance on "social recall" for record-keeping.
- **Internal Conflicts:** Governance reports (CLEEN 2026) document frequent leadership crises in town unions, often triggered by disputes over meeting minutes or financial expenditures that were not transparently recorded.
- **Elite Capture:** The "Recall Trap" allows dominant leaders to alter decisions post-hoc, leading to corruption and embezzlement of community funds (Leadership Crisis Study 2019).
- **Insecurity and Opaque Processes:** Lack of verifiable artifacts prevents effective coordination with state security forces, as community norms and member registries are often non-legible to external partners.

## 4.3 The Institutional Stagnation Barrier
We observe that while town unions are highly effective at mobilizing social capital for *physical* infrastructure, they struggle to sustain *digital* infrastructure. This is not due to a lack of interest, but because the "social recall" model does not scale to the management of complex digital resources. This empirical reality supports our claim that digital transformation must begin with the stabilization of institutional memory.
