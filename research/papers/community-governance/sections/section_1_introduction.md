# Section 1: Introduction — The Missing Tier of Digital Governance

Mainstream digital governance research has a blind spot: the state. By viewing e-government primarily as a relationship between formal state bureaucracies and individual citizens, current frameworks overlook the "Missing Tier"—the informal community institutions that already perform the bulk of governance functions in much of the Global South. In Southeast Nigeria, town unions, age grades, and women's associations are the "principal agents" of development (SFJESGS 2021), providing security and infrastructure where the state presence is "thin."

This paper argues that these informal institutions are not "pre-modern" relics but are, in fact, the most resilient engines of digital transformation. However, they are currently constrained by a "coordination ceiling" imposed by their reliance on social recall. We propose a conceptual framework for community-led digital governance that transitions from "recall-based trust" to "artifact-based trust" without breaking the moral legitimacy of these institutions.

## 1.1 The Research Problem: The Failure of State-Centricity
Why has digital transformation in sub-Saharan Africa struggled to reach the "last mile"? We argue that it is due to a "State-Centric Bias" (Effoduh 2024) that tries to digitize centralized bureaucracies while ignoring the polycentric governance centers where collective action actually happens. This bias leads to "isomorphic mimicry"—building digital portals that look like state governance but lack the local social capital to function.

## 1.2 Objective and Method
The objective of this research is to construct a theoretical framework that accounts for the unique governance logic of community institutions. Following Jabareen (2009), we synthesize principles from **Polycentricity**, **Procedural Justice**, and **Institutional Legitimacy** to produce a model for community-led digital transformation.

## 1.3 Contribution
This paper makes a primary contribution to the "Global South" track of digital governance by:
1. Identifying the "Informal Institution Gap" in mainstream e-government frameworks.
2. Proposing the **"Community Coordination Framework"**, which reframes digital artifacts as the anchors for informal institutional memory.
3. Providing empirical evidence from Southeast Nigeria to demonstrate the potential of town unions as governance actors.

By centering the community institution, rather than the state, as the primary unit of digital transformation, this research provides a roadmap for more resilient and culturally grounded digital public infrastructure.
