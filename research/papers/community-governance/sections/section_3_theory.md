# Section 3: Theoretical Framework — Polycentricity and Procedural Justice

This paper constructs a conceptual framework for community digital governance by synthesizing three core theoretical pillars: **Polycentric Governance** (Ostrom 1990), **Procedural Justice** (Tyler 2006), and **Institutional Legitimacy** (Beetham 1991, Suchman 1995). Following the 8-phase procedure for conceptual framework building (Jabareen 2009), this framework aims to explain how informal community institutions in Southeast Nigeria can act as engines of digital transformation.

## 3.1 Polycentricity as an Organizational Primitive
Ostrom’s polycentricity (1990, 2010) provides the structural foundation. Unlike state-centric models that view governance as a top-down hierarchy, polycentricity acknowledges multiple, overlapping centers of decision-making that operate independently yet are nested within a larger system. We argue that Southeast Nigerian town unions are classic polycentric actors—they exercise autonomous legislative, executive, and judicial powers over their members while interacting with (and often filling the gaps of) the local government system.

## 3.2 Procedural Justice and the "Trust-Artifact" Link
While polycentricity describes the *structure*, Procedural Justice (Tyler 2006) describes the *mechanism* of compliance. Tyler argues that people comply with rules not out of fear of punishment (instrumental motive) but because they perceive the decision-making process as fair (normative motive). In community governance, this fairness is historically anchored in "social recall." We propose that digital transformation in this context must transition from "social recall" to "artifact-based evidence" without breaking the procedural justice loop. 

## 3.3 The Legitimacy Paradox in Informal Institutions
Suchman (1995) defines legitimacy as the generalized perception that the actions of an entity are desirable, proper, or appropriate. Informal institutions like town unions often possess high **moral legitimacy** (rooted in community values) but low **cognitive legitimacy** (not taken for granted as formal governance actors by e-government frameworks). This "legitimacy paradox" explains why mainstream digital governance projects often bypass town unions, leading to "state-centric" failure.

## 3.4 The Synthesized Framework: Community Digital Sovereignty
The synthesized framework (Figure 1) posits that for a community institution to drive digital transformation, it must satisfy three conditions:
1. **Structural Autonomy:** The ability to define and enforce local digital norms (Polycentricity).
2. **Procedural Transparency:** The use of digital artifacts to validate fairness (Procedural Justice).
3. **Institutional Legibility:** The translation of informal authority into formats recognized by formal e-government systems (Legitimacy).
