# Section 5: Discussion — Design Implications for E-Government

The synthesis of our theoretical framework (Section 3) and empirical observations (Section 4) leads to several critical design implications for digital governance in the Global South.

## 5.1 From State-Led Portals to Community-Led Infrastructure
Current e-government strategies prioritize the creation of central state portals. Our framework suggests that a more resilient approach is to build "Community-Led Infrastructure" that anchors digital records at the community level. By providing town unions with tools to manage their own registries, treasury state transitions, and deliberative records, the state can leverage existing social capital rather than trying to centralize all governance functions.

## 5.2 The "Legibility" Requirement
For community governance to scale, it must become "legible" to the state. This does not mean formalizing town unions into state bureaucracies, but rather providing them with digital artifacts that use standard formats (e.g., verifiable credentials for membership). This "middle-out" legibility allows for interoperability between community institutions and state-level e-government systems.

## 5.3 Risks: Over-Formalization and Digital Exclusion
Transitioning from "social recall" to digital artifacts carries risks:
1. **The Over-Formalization Trap:** Rigid digital systems may stifle the flexibility that makes informal institutions resilient. Design must allow for "soft" norms and informal deliberation before final state transitions are anchored.
2. **The Digital Divide:** If digital coordination is restricted to those with high-end devices, it may exacerbate elite capture. Systems must be "multi-modal," supporting low-bandwidth and offline interaction.

## 5.4 Reframing Accountability: Artifacts as "Receipts of Trust"
Our framework reframes accountability not as top-down state audit, but as horizontal community transparency. Digital artifacts act as "receipts of trust" that allow any member to verify that a decision made in an assembly matches the record on the ledger. This bottom-up accountability is more likely to be accepted by community members than external state-imposed regulations.
